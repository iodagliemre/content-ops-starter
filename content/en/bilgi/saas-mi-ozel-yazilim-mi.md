# SaaS or custom software? The right model for your business

Every business reaches the same question at some point: should I subscribe to a ready-made program, or have my own system built? Both are legitimate choices. The mistake is never asking the question, signing up for the first subscription you find, and discovering five years later that your critical processes are locked inside someone else's product.

## What SaaS gives, and what it takes

SaaS (Software as a Service) is ready-made software you use for a monthly or yearly fee. Accounting software, booking apps, CRMs — most of them work this way.

**What it gives:** A fast start, a low initial cost, maintenance and updates handled by the provider. For a small team, signing up today and using it tomorrow is a real advantage.

**What it takes:** The software is not yours. Your data sits on the provider's servers. Your workflow bends only as far as the program allows. The price is reset every year, and you are not the one setting it. If the provider shuts down, is acquired or changes the product, your process changes too.

## What custom software gives, and what it takes

Custom software is a system designed around your business's own processes, and owned by you. The code, the data and the infrastructure are your property.

**What it gives:** Full control. The program fits the workflow, not the other way round. The data stays in your own infrastructure. You pay once; after that it is maintenance and development, not rent. The system gains value as it grows; institutional memory builds up.

**What it takes:** A higher starting cost. Setup takes weeks, not days. You need a partner for maintenance; you choose a partner, not a provider.

## Which one, when?

**SaaS makes sense:** When the process is standard and everyone in the sector does it the same way. When you are testing a market. When the team is small and nobody wants to deal with maintenance. When use is short-term or seasonal.

**Custom software makes sense:** When the process is what sets you apart from competitors. When regulation is heavy and data sovereignty is a must (healthcare, occupational health and safety, finance). When institutional memory has to stay in your own system. When the investment is long-term and you want to draw the roadmap yourself. When platform limits break your workflow.

## Three questions to decide

1. **Is this process critical?** If it stops, does the business stop? If it is critical, control should be yours.
2. **Should this data stay ours?** Patient records, customer history, company documents — is it a risk for them to sit on someone else's server?
3. **What happens in five years?** What does the subscription add up to over five years? If the provider changes, how painful is the move?

If your answer to all three is "yes, ours, painful", the answer is clear.
